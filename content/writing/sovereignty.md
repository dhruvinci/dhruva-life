---
title: Sovereignty
date: 2022-02-20
excerpt: "I own a copy of Kind of Blue. I don't own a single song on my phone. That difference is why I'm in crypto."
---

I was cleaning my copy of Kind of Blue this morning, slowly, the way you do when there's nowhere to be, and it struck me that it's one of the few things I own in the full sense of the word.

Nobody can take it off my shelf because a licensing deal expired. Nobody can change the terms on it overnight. If I want to lend it to a friend, I lend it. If I want to sell it, I sell it. If some company decides next year that jazz isn't profitable, it still spins. The Kiasmos Blurred EP next to it, the same. Bonobo's Migration, the same. Meanwhile, I don't own a single song on my phone. I rent access, and the landlord can change the rules whenever they like.

That little gap, between owning something and being allowed to use it, is most of why I'm in crypto.

I know what crypto looks like from the outside right now. It looks like charts and apes and people shouting about hundred-x returns. I get why people roll their eyes. There's a lot of noise, some of it is pure speculation, and some of it is worse than that. I'm not going to defend all of it. But that's the loudest part of the room, not the most interesting part, and I think it's worth saying out loud what keeps people like me here after the noise.

For me it's sovereignty. It's a big word and I mean it in a very plain way. Can you hold the thing that's yours, without asking anyone's permission?

Almost everything in our digital lives is held for us. Our money sits in accounts that can be frozen, our photos sit on servers that can be shut down, our identities sit in a login someone else controls, and our work sits on platforms that can change the algorithm and quietly erase years of effort. Most of the time this is fine, because most of the time the people holding our stuff are decent and the systems work. But "most of the time" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

Self-custody flips that around. When I hold my own keys, the thing is mine. It's a slightly terrifying feeling the first time. There's no "forgot password" button, no customer support to call, no bank manager who'll sort it out. You write down a phrase, you store it somewhere safe, and you realise you are now fully responsible. It's the same feeling I had leaving home for Syracuse, or leaving PwC last year. Freedom and responsibility arrive in the same box. You don't get to order just one.

And I think that's a good thing. Financial independence isn't only about having money, it's about not needing permission to use what you have. For a lot of people in the world, including plenty of people in the country I just moved back to, that decides whether they get to take part in the global economy at all.

The part that gets me most excited, though, is what happens when a lot of sovereign people join the same network.

In game theory, which is what I spent a good chunk of my Masters on, you learn that the shape of the game decides how people behave far more than their intentions do. Most of the internet we grew up with is built as a zero-sum game. The platform wins by capturing value from the users and the creators, and everyone's fighting over the same scraps of attention. Crypto networks, when they're designed well, can be positive-sum. The early users get a stake, the creators get paid directly, the community that makes a thing valuable actually owns a piece of that value. When the network grows, everyone in it grows. You don't have to trust anyone to be nice. The incentives do the work.

My purpose in life is to play positive sum games. Life is just a game. And it's important to keep playing. I've believed that for a long time, and I keep finding it again in this space.

I don't think any of this is guaranteed. The same technology can be used to build casinos, and a lot of people are building casinos (it's a bull market, what did we expect). Holding your own keys can go badly if you're careless. Positive-sum networks can turn extractive the moment the wrong people control the incentives. I'd be lying if I said I knew how it all turns out.

But I know what I want to build towards. An internet where you can own your stuff the way I own that record. Where leaving a platform doesn't mean leaving everything behind. Where the people who show up and make something valuable get to keep a piece of it.

The record's back on the shelf now. Still mine.
