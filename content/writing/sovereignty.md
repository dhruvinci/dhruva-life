---
title: Sovereignty
date: 2022-02-20
excerpt: "I own a copy of Kind of Blue. I don't own a single song on my phone. That difference is why I'm in crypto."
---

I own a copy of Kind of Blue. It sits on a shelf, it collects a bit of dust, and every now and then I clean it and play it and it sounds exactly the way it did the first time.

Nobody can take it off my shelf because a licensing deal expired. Nobody can change the terms on it overnight. If I want to lend it to a friend, I lend it. If I want to sell it, I sell it. If some company decides next year that jazz isn't profitable, it still spins.

Meanwhile I don't own a single song on my phone. I rent access, and the landlord can change the rules whenever they like.

That little gap, between owning something and being allowed to use it, is most of why I'm in crypto.

I know what crypto looks like from the outside right now. It looks like charts and cartoon apes and people shouting about hundred-x returns. I get the eye rolls. There's a lot of noise, a lot of it is speculation, and some of it is worse than that. I'm not going to defend all of it. But that's the loudest part of the room, not the most interesting part.

For me it's sovereignty. It's a big word and I mean it in a very plain way. Can you hold the thing that's yours, without asking anyone's permission?

Almost everything in our digital lives is held for us. Our money sits in accounts that can be frozen. Our photos sit on servers that can be shut down. Our identity is a login someone else controls, and our work lives on platforms that can change the algorithm and quietly erase years of effort. Most of the time this is fine, because most of the time the people holding our stuff are decent and the systems work. But "most of the time" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

Self-custody flips it around. The first time you hold your own keys, it's a slightly terrifying feeling. There's no forgot-password button, no customer support, no bank manager who'll sort it out. You write down a phrase, you hide it somewhere, and you realise you are now completely responsible for this thing. Freedom and responsibility arrive in the same box. You don't get to order just one.

I think that's a good trade. Financial independence isn't only about having money. It's about not needing permission to use what you have. For a lot of people in the world, including a lot of people in this country, that decides whether they get to take part at all.

The part that gets me most excited is what happens when a lot of sovereign people join the same network.

The internet we grew up with is mostly a zero-sum game. The platform wins by capturing value from the people using it, and everyone fights over the same scraps of attention. Crypto networks, when they're designed well, don't have to work like that. The early users can own a stake. Creators can get paid directly. The community that makes something valuable can actually own a piece of that value, so when the network grows, everyone in it grows. You don't have to trust anyone to be nice. The incentives do the work.

"When they're designed well" is doing some heavy lifting of its own, I know. The same technology builds casinos, and right now a lot of people are building casinos (it's a bull market, what did we expect). Holding your own keys can go badly if you're careless. A network can turn extractive the moment the wrong people end up controlling the incentives. I'd be lying if I said I knew how this plays out.

But I know which internet I'd rather live on. One where you can own your stuff the way I own that record. Where leaving a platform doesn't mean leaving everything behind.

The record's back on the shelf. Still mine.
