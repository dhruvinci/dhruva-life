---
title: One Click
date: 2022-08-21
excerpt: "The best answer to \"what is an NFT?\" turned out to be: click here."
---

For most of this year, the question I've heard more than any other is "wait, what's an NFT?"

People ask it at dinner, in client meetings, in DMs from relatives. Some people know they can make money off of them, and some don't, and almost nobody can explain what one actually is without a whiteboard and twenty minutes. The wallets are confusing, the gas fees are confusing, the words are confusing. Every step between a curious person and actually owning one is a step where they give up.

So we asked ourselves a slightly different question. What if you didn't have to understand any of it?

The project we built was an open edition - a free mint that anyone, anywhere in the world, could claim in one click. Put the link in a tweet, an email, a message, anything. Click it, and a few clicks later, you own an NFT. We abstracted away almost everything: the blockchain interactions, the smart contract calls, even most of the regular web friction you'd expect. You didn't need to know what was happening underneath. You just needed to want the thing.

And that was the real idea, I think. Stop making people care about the token. Make them care about the thing being collected. Nobody who collects stamps talks about the glue.

We knew this was the goal we were working towards. We did not expect what happened next.

Within four days, over a million mints. A million. At a time when most people still couldn't tell you what an NFT was, a million of them clicked a link and walked away owning one. I still don't fully have words for it. It's the kind of number you stare at on a dashboard and then refresh, just in case.

What I'm proudest of, honestly, is how little broke. We'd tested this to death before launch, and it showed. The smart contracts held. The flows held. The only real fires were the boring, beautiful kind - managing servers and a flood of requests we'd planned for, just not quite this big. My team spent those four days scaling things up and grinning at each other, not scrambling to fix a bug in front of a million people. That's a very different kind of launch week, and I don't take it for granted.

The funny part is how fast it stopped being special. It's only been a few months and already one-click, free, abstracted mints are starting to feel like the obvious way to do it. Other people are doing it, and doing it well. Nobody asks how it's possible anymore, they just expect it.

I used to think that would bother me. It doesn't. That's what it looks like when something you built changes how things are done - it gets taken for granted. Nobody thanks the first person who made a door handle easy to turn. They just stop thinking about doors.

That's the kind of innovation I want to keep making. The kind that disappears into how things work.

Click here. That's the whole answer.
