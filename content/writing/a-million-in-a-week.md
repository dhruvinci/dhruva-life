---
title: A Million in a Week
date: 2022-08-21
excerpt: "It was buggy, the deadline wouldn't move, and then a million people showed up in a week."
---

I'm still not over it, so I'm going to try and write it down before it turns into a story I tell at dinner.

A few weeks before launch, it didn't look like a launch. It looked like a list. Bugs we knew about, bugs we suspected, things the client wanted that we'd said yes to a little too quickly, and a date that wasn't moving no matter how politely we looked at it. The kind of stretch where you stop counting days and start counting hours of sleep, and neither number is very big.

I remember thinking, more than once, that we'd bitten off something we couldn't chew.

This is the part people don't see from the outside. Web3 launches look clean on Twitter. A countdown, an announcement, a slick video. Behind it is a team of mostly young builders, some of them in their first real job, fixing a smart contract flow at an hour when their families think they're asleep. Someone rewriting the same piece of code again because the last version wasn't holding up. Someone else on a call with the client's team, calmly explaining why the thing they want tomorrow will take till Thursday. And me, jumping between all of it, doing the architecture in my head while I'm also doing the partnership on the phone, trying to look calmer than I felt.

I wear my feelings on my face, so I'm not sure how well I hid that last part.

Then we launched.

For the first few hours it was nerves. Is it holding, is it slow, why is that error there, who's on it. Then people started coming in, and then more, and then the numbers stopped looking like numbers we'd planned for. By the end of the week we'd onboarded a million users.

A million people. A lot of them, I'd guess, touching anything like this for the first time.

Whenever someone asks me how it went, I end up saying some version of the same sentence. It's buggy, we're dancing with deadlines, and then suddenly we launch and onboard a million users in a week - it takes my breath away.

It still does.

I want to be honest about the "buggy" part, because it would be easy to leave it out now that it worked. It wasn't perfect. There were rough edges, there were fixes pushed during the week while real people were using it, and there were things I'd do differently with more time. But I've started to believe that if you wait until it's perfect, you never get the week. You have to be willing to ship something slightly embarrassing and then show up every hour to make it less so. That's what the team did. They didn't just build it, they stayed with it.

What I'll remember isn't the number, though. It's the feeling in the team when it became real. People who'd been quietly exhausted suddenly had this lit-up look, sharing screenshots, reading user messages out loud, laughing at the dumb bugs we'd been scared of a week earlier. For a few days, nobody really wanted to go home.

I don't know if we'll ever have a week like that again. Maybe we'll have bigger ones, maybe this was the one. Either way, I got to stand in the middle of it with people I love working with, all of us giving it everything we had.

I slept a lot the week after. Worth it.
