---
title: When to Stop Playing
date: 2023-09-10
excerpt: "For a while there, we were making more money than the value we were providing. I've been sitting with that."
---

Honestly, some of the money we made in the bull market wasn't ours to be proud of.

I've been sitting with that sentence for a few weeks now, trying to decide whether to write it down, because it's not the kind of thing founders usually say out loud. But I think if I don't say it I'll start telling myself a nicer story, and nicer stories are how you end up making the same mistake twice.

When we started, the market was on fire. Every brand wanted an NFT strategy, every agency deck had the word "metaverse" on slide two, and budgets were being approved for things nobody fully understood, including, at times, us. We were good at what we did, and I'm proud of the work. But if I'm being straight with myself, the market was paying us for the mood as much as for the product. Dehidden scaled too fast in the bull market - we were making more money than the value we were providing.

That gap is dangerous, and it's dangerous in a sneaky way, because from the inside it feels exactly like success. Revenue's up, the team's growing, clients are calling you. Nobody sends you an email saying "hey, just so you know, a big chunk of this is the market being silly." You have to notice it yourself. And you mostly notice it when the tide goes out and the same pitch that used to close quickly now drags on for months, or doesn't close at all, and you realise a good chunk of what you thought was your skill was just the weather.

I've sort of turned one idea into a personal rule over the years. My purpose in life is to play positive sum games. Life is just a game. And it's important to keep playing. The deals I love most are the ones where everyone walks away better - the brand gets a community that actually cares, the users get something fun and real, we get paid for creating that. When it works like that, you want to play forever.

But a bull market bends the maths. When the value you're being paid for is bigger than the value you're creating, somebody on the other side is losing, even if they don't know it yet. Maybe it's the brand that spent money on something that won't last. Maybe it's the user holding a token they were told meant more than it did. The game stops being positive-sum and quietly becomes something else, and you're still smiling on the call because the numbers look fine.

I don't think we did anything wrong, exactly. We built what people asked for and we built it well. What I'm less comfortable with is how long it took me to see the gap clearly, and how much I wanted to not see it.

So the lesson I'm taking, and it's the one I think will matter most for the rest of my life, is about knowing when to stop playing a particular game. Not life, not building, not the big game. A particular one. The version of a market or a business model or a role that used to be positive-sum and isn't anymore. Everyone talks about grit and never quitting (I'm guilty of this, I love that stuff), but there's a different kind of courage in looking at a game you're winning and asking whether it's still a good game.

There's a small list of questions I've started asking myself, usually late at night:

Would the people paying us be happy with what they got, if the hype disappeared tomorrow?
Would I still build this if the market cared a lot less?
Is everyone at the table still having fun?

When the answers start going the wrong way, that's a signal. It doesn't mean panic, and it doesn't mean burn it all down. It means pay attention, start steering, and be honest with yourself about what you're actually being paid for.

I don't know yet where the steering takes us. I just know I'd rather see the gap than pretend it isn't there.

I'd rather know than not know.
