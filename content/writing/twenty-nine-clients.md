Somewhere around the twentieth kickoff call, I realised I could finish the brand's sentences for them.

"We want to reward our community." Yes. "But we don't want it to feel like a crypto thing." Of course. "Can people claim it without a wallet?" They can. "And can it change over time, like, unlock something later?" It can, we've done that, let me show you. By the time the call ended I'd usually sketched the whole architecture in my head, and a lot of the time it looked almost exactly like the one we'd built the month before for somebody else.

We've now worked with 29 brands at Dehidden. Some of them are names you'd recognise - Adidas x Prada, Mercedes, Polygon Studios - and some are smaller teams who just wanted to try something new with their community. Every one of them felt special at the start. Every one had a different logo, a different launch date, a different person on their side who was very excited and slightly terrified. And every one, once you peeled off the branding, was asking us for some combination of the same handful of things: mint something, gate something, make it change, make it easy to claim, make people come back.

I don't think I would've seen that as quickly if it weren't for PwC.

For four years there, my job was basically to walk into large companies with a sprawling mess of tools and figure out what they actually needed. Salesforce talking to MuleSoft talking to some ancient system that nobody wanted to touch, three teams building the same thing in three different ways, mergers where two companies had to become one without the lights going off. What that job trains into you, after a while, is a kind of squint. You stop looking at the specific request and start looking at the shape of it. Which bits are genuinely unique to this client, and which bits are the same problem wearing a different jacket. It's product and solution architecture, really, and I didn't know I'd be leaning on it this hard in a startup selling NFTs (Web3 and metaverses lol).

So at Dehidden I ended up owning every client partnership and every product delivery, partly because that squint was useful and partly because, honestly, I couldn't let go. And the squint kept telling me the same thing. We weren't running 29 different projects. We were building one product 29 times, by hand.

That's fine when you're starting out. Client work is a brilliant teacher. You get paid to learn, you get real users on day one, and you find out very quickly what brands care about versus what crypto Twitter cares about (those are very different lists). Our enterprise minting suite and Quests, the engage-to-earn product with dynamic and soulbound NFTs, both came out of that - we kept building similar things for clients until they hardened into tools. Token-gated access, dynamic metadata, airdrops at scale. We pioneered a lot of it because clients kept asking and we kept saying yes.

But client work has a ceiling, and it's you. Every new brand meant another kickoff, another scoping doc, another few weeks of my team rebuilding pieces that already existed, another launch night. And launch nights are addictive, I won't pretend otherwise. It's buggy, we're dancing with deadlines, and then suddenly we launch and onboard a million users in a week - it takes my breath away. But you can't build a company on adrenaline and a calendar full of kickoffs. At some point you're just a very enthusiastic agency.

So we're doing the thing the patterns have been telling us to do. We're building a self-serve product that brands can use themselves. Take everything we've done 29 times, pull out the parts that were always the same, and give it to the brand directly, so the person on their side who was excited and slightly terrified can just go and do it, without waiting on us.

It's a strange feeling, building the thing that makes your own services less necessary. Part of me feels like I'm automating myself out of the room. But the bigger part of me thinks that's exactly the point. If we're really good at this, the best version of our knowledge shouldn't live in my head or on my team's calls. It should live in a product that anyone can pick up at 11pm on a Tuesday and have a campaign live by morning.

The hardest bit isn't technical. It's deciding what to leave out. Every client had one special request, and if we built all 29 of them we'd end up with a product nobody can use. The squint helps here too.

Self-serve is a very different game from client work - no hand-holding, no charm on a call, the product has to explain itself. But I'd rather bet on the pattern than keep being the pattern.

Twenty-nine was enough. Time to build the thirtieth once.
