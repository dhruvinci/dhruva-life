---
title: Handing It Over
date: 2023-12-17
excerpt: "Polygon acquired Dehidden, and the thing they wanted was the product we built so brands wouldn't need us."
---

It's done. Polygon has acquired Dehidden.

I've typed and deleted that line more times than I'd like to admit, because every version of it sounded either too casual or too dramatic, and the truth is it feels like both. Two and a bit years of my life fit into one sentence now. That's a strange thing to look at.

The part that makes me smile the most is why it happened. Earlier this year I wrote about how, after 29 clients, the patterns had become impossible to ignore, and how we decided to take everything we'd built by hand for brands and turn it into a self-serve product they could use themselves. At the time it felt like a slightly risky bet. We were good at client work, we knew how to win a room and ship a launch, and here we were building the thing that would make us less necessary. That self-serve product is the reason Polygon wanted Dehidden. The thing we built so brands wouldn't need us is the thing that found us a home.

I keep thinking about that. You spend so much energy trying to make yourself indispensable, and then the most valuable thing you make is the thing that works without you.

Let me go back a bit, because I want to remember this properly.

In 2021 I was an enterprise architect at PwC in the US, four years in, on what everyone would call the safe path. I'd gone deep into crypto on my own time, and I couldn't stop thinking about it, so I started Dehidden in August while I was still at PwC, left in November, and moved back to Bangalore to build. I had a lot of conviction and not much of a plan. Web3 then was the frontier (Web3 and metaverses lol, I know, I know), and it genuinely felt like the rules of the internet were being rewritten and I wanted to be one of the people writing them.

What I'm proud of, in no particular order.

We built real things for real brands. Adidas x Prada, Mercedes, Polygon Studios, and many more who trusted a young team from Bangalore with their name on the line. We built an enterprise minting suite and Quests, an engage-to-earn product with dynamic and soulbound NFTs, and we were early on things that are table stakes now - token-gated access, dynamic metadata, airdrops that actually worked at scale. We had a launch that onboarded a million users in a week, and I don't think I'll ever forget the feeling of watching those numbers climb while half the team was running on chai and no sleep.

We gave young builders a real shot. People who came in with hunger instead of CVs left as engineers and product people I'd trust with anything, and some of them have gone on to start their own companies. If I had to pick one thing to be remembered for from this chapter, it'd be that, over any product.

And I'm proud of the honesty, even the uncomfortable bits. The bull market let us make more money than the value we were providing, and I said so, and we changed direction because of it. I learned when to stop playing a game that had stopped being positive-sum. That lesson cost something. I think it was worth it.

What it feels like, honestly, is a mix. There's relief, the kind you only notice after you put down something heavy you'd forgotten you were carrying. There's pride. There's a small grief too, the way you feel when you move out of a flat you loved - the rooms are still there, somebody else is going to live in them, and that's good, but it's not yours anymore. I wear my heart on a sleeve, and I'm a very animated person, so anyone who's spoken to me this week has probably seen all three of those in about ten minutes.

Handing over a product you built is a weird act of trust. You're saying, here, this is my way of seeing a problem, written down in code and decisions and a hundred small trade-offs, please look after it. I'm glad it's going somewhere that understands what it's for.

I don't know what's next yet, and for once I'm not rushing to find out. For the last two years I've been running at full speed, every client, every delivery, every launch night. I'd like to stop for a moment, breathe, and see what I actually want to obsess over when nobody's asking me to.

The chapter's closed. I read every page.
