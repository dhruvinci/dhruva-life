---
title: A Coach That Watches
date: 2025-06-22
excerpt: "I pointed a vision model at a jiu-jitsu match to see what it understood. Mostly, it didn't."
---

Honestly, it didn't work. Not even close.

I've spent a few evenings pointing open vision-language models at jiu-jitsu footage, mostly Andrew Tackett matches because I've watched them so many times I could narrate them in my sleep. I tried LLaVA, I tried Qwen2.5-VL, I cut clips short and long, I asked nicely and I asked very specifically. The answers came back fluent and confident and grammatical, and a lot of them were just wrong. Which leg belongs to which person, who's on top, whether that's a guard or a pass in progress - the moment two bodies are tangled up, the models lose the plot.

In fairness, that's the hardest thing you could ask them. Jiu-jitsu is two people wrapped around each other, half the limbs hidden at any given moment, and most of what matters is invisible in a single frame. A grip that's about to break. Weight that's about to shift. It's chess with your body as the pieces, except in 4D, because the fourth dimension is time. A position doesn't mean much on its own, it's what came before it and what's about to come after that tells you whether you're winning. Show a model one still frame and ask it who's winning and you might as well be showing it a single chess move with the board covered.

So why was I doing this at all? Mostly curiosity. I've been thinking a lot about how models read ambiguous pictures, and nothing is more ambiguous than two people wrapped around each other. The footage I know best is from the mats, so that's where I pointed it.

But there's a selfish reason too. Every time I roll, there are moments I can't explain afterwards. I got swept and I don't know how. I passed and I don't know why it worked that time and not the ten before. You can film your rolls, but rewatching them alone only helps so much when you don't know what you're looking for. What I actually want is a coach who's watched every roll I've ever done and can tell me the pattern I keep falling into. When I first tried to put it into words, the best I could come up with was "an AI jiu jitsu critique and mentor basically." Not very poetic, but that's the idea.

Seeing the models fail made that idea more interesting to me, not less. If they were already great at this, it'd be a product problem, and there'd be ten apps doing it by now. The fact that they fall apart on two people in contact means there's something real and unsolved in there, something about bodies and occlusion and time that these models just don't have yet. That's the kind of problem I can't leave alone.

I want to be clear with myself that this is curiosity, not a company. It's an itch. I'll probably keep poking at it the way I poke at a position I keep losing in - try something, fail, go home, think about it in the shower, try something slightly different next time.

The mats taught me that the stuck feeling is usually right before something opens up.

Let's see.
