Sure, when we started, code came out buggy, models hallucinated, and prompts had to be finely engineered. But over the course of the year we've seen leaps of improvement in both the models and vibe coding tools. Show me a developer who can improve that quickly - that's Karpathy's lament.

2025 has been the year of vibe coding, and 2026 will only accelerate this further.

## 2. Do you know your AI's personality type?

Do you know your AI's personality type?

If you've been using AI for a long time you'll agree that they have personalities.

And if you're building a team of them, you need to recruit for the right roles.

ChatGPT is your EA or PM. remembers your conversations, tracks context across projects, agentic by nature, ticks boxes and follows up.

Claude is your Lead Developer. Opus for deep coding sessions. Haiku for quick debugging. Sonnet for architecture discussions. Give it your technical thinking.

Perplexity is your Research Analyst. Searches, synthesizes, cites. Doesn't hallucinate as much because it's grounded in sources. Give it your rabbit holes.

Gemini is your BA. Lives inside your Google ecosystem. Analyzes sheets, reads docs, searches the web in one breath. Give it your data work.

And your on-device open source model is the one that works with you when you're on a flight or in the middle of the ocean and don't have your internet :P

All of them replace your Search Engine for simple queries. 

And none of them replace human judgment for complex decisions.

The real unlock isn't picking the "best" model; it's designing how they hand off to each other. What data flows where, which model owns which task, and where a human stays in the loop.

You're not choosing a tool anymore. You're architecting your dream team. And chemistry is key.

Keep that in mind when you're building your next AI solution.

## 3. What if I told you your AI doesn't understand you? And that it was designed that

What if I told you your AI doesn't understand you? And that it was designed that way…

When you ask ChatGPT a question, it's not comprehending your intent. It's calculating the most statistically probable next token based on patterns in its training data.

So it generates. Confidently. And quite often wrongly.

But it was designed that way! When the model doesn't know something, it has two options:

1. Admit uncertainty (which it was rarely rewarded for)
2. Generate something plausible (which got positive feedback constantly)

That’s because these AI companies are incentivised by you believing what the AI tells you and thinking it is correct and smart.

How would you know if you rarely cross check everything the AI tells you? How would you do that if you weren’t an expert on the matter?

Ask it something outside the distribution. Push it into edge cases. Watch it confidently generate nonsense with the same tone it uses for facts. We’ve already seen these examples with previous models - remember the ‘STRAWBERRY’ test? Models are plagued with fallacies like those.

So the model isn’t thinking. It's pattern-matching at scale. This works remarkably well, until it doesn't.

You can fix some of it with prompt engineering and asking good questions. But the fundamental fix is rethinking how we train models to be honest about the boundaries of their own knowledge.

We need AIs that know what they don't know.

## 4. There is a growing need for building unbiased AIs.

There is a growing need for building unbiased AIs.

When I was majoring in AI during my Masters' in 2017, I asked my professor if there were any studies around how to factor for the biases of the humans who build the models. He said there wasn't, and that scared me. 8 years later, and that problem still hasn't been solved.

A study by Berkeley shows that most AIs tend to be biased towards left leaning Liberal values and we still don't know if this is a bug or a feature. However, there are a lot of thought exercises going into how to build an unbiased AI.

There are primarily two schools of thoughts:

1. Atomic Unbiasedness: 
> This is essentially attempting what philosopher Thomas Nagel called "The View from Nowhere". Where we try and bring about unbiasedness in every single aspect of thought. The idea of a perspective-less, purely objective standpoint.
> But this fails in practice because it hard to train by omission, all data contain some degree of bias, and even the action of selecting which topics to address is a form of bias. 
> Nietzsche says "knowledge from no point of view is as incoherent a notion as seeing from no particular vantage point." You can't see a building without standing somewhere. You can't know something without a frame of reference.

2. The Dynamic Center of Bias:
> In this theory we try and train a model on ALL forms of bias and then find a way to weight it out so it is equally biased towards everything, thereby being paradoxically unbiased. And the center isn't fixed; it emerges from the balance of represented perspectives.
> Berkeley's study aligns with this proposal wherein: "when asked about a controversial topic, the machine should produce an answer which 1) as many people as possible from each side of a debate would say fairly includes their perspective while 2) ensuring that the same percentage of each side approves."
> But this is also hard in practice since biases aren't uniform in magnitude or type. Some are factual errors. Some are value differences. Some are framing choices. "Equally biased toward everything" is as hard to specify as "unbiased about everything."

I think both are interesting approaches. But I think both are wrong.

I think the goal isn't a view from nowhere or perfectly balanced biases, but legible partiality that users can reason about.

Trying to create certainty in something as uncertain as 'unbiasedness' is a fallacy in itself. The best we can (and should) build for is a system of transparency, steerability, and adversarial checking, so the models can grow as we do too. 

Food for thought.

## 5. Everyone's talking about an AI bubble. That might apply to LLMs and their wrappe

Everyone's talking about an AI bubble. That might apply to LLMs and their wrapper products. Meanwhile, there's silent and slow growth happening in a space most people haven't even heard of - Vision Language Models (VLMs).

These aren't just the simple image/video generators like Sora or Veo, but models that can watch video and actually understand what's happening, and do a lot more with that visual knowledge.

Here's what most people miss about VLMs:
1. They don't just "see" images. They reason across time, tracking objects, actions, and context frame by frame. Time is the key variable and we are still working on making models pay attention for a long time. 
2. They're not just video generators; that's just one side of VLMs that doesn't paint the whole picture. The hard problem isn't making video, it's understanding it with so many other real world elements like mathematics and physics, and knowing what's happening across frames like our brains instinctively do.
3. They're already outperforming traditional CV (Computer Vision) on complex tasks like action recognition, anomaly detection, and temporal reasoning, but they often work hand-in-hand with these tools. So architectures are complicated and wrapper products are not so easy to build.
4. Token economics are brutal. A 60-second video can be 1000x more expensive to process than a text prompt because of how much more data we carry across these frames. A picture does say a 1000 words and 60 seconds are about 1440 frames. Do the math.
5. Benchmarks are broken. We've only evaluated video generation with the 'Will Smith eating Spaghetti' test and we're still in the process of building comprehensive evals for both quantitative and qualitative measurable outputs.
6. Understanding humans is far more complicated than we think. We instinctively know how humans look and move, even in highly occluded scenarios, but VLMs struggle here a lot. Remember all those generated images of people with the wrong number of fingers? Scale that for a long video.
7. Long-horizon understanding is still in the works. Most models lose coherence after 20+ seconds, which is often the length limit of videos we see generated, and currently we're working on understanding 20+ minute videos with consistent high attention.
8. This is where the moat will be built. Whoever solves temporal reasoning at scale wins the next decade of multimodal AI. And it's not going to be easy.

I've spent most of this year researching and building in this space. And I'll be sharing a lot of my learnings with y'all as I go along.
The gap between product demos and production-ready VLMs is massive.
But the upside? Even bigger.
I've been working on use cases in the realms of sports and public safety and I can't wait to share them with you!

## 6. AIs are smart, but they miss one key variable - time.

AIs are smart, but they miss one key variable - time.

If you've tried coding something or even had a conversation with AI, I'm sure you've been frustrated by how quickly it forgets things. Even the best AIs have the memory of a goldfish, and use memory orchestration as a bandaid to try and give you the illusion of memory.

Claude Code forgets context within 10 minutes of coding, Gemini treats every conversation as a fresh start, and ChatGPT remembers maybe 3 key things from each conversation at best. And that's opened up opportunities for MCPs to try and solve this by creating a directory of note files to solve this. But it's just not good enough.

This problem is imperative to solve when it comes to vision - if you want to count objects in an image, no problem. But ask it to remember what's happening over the course of a video and they quickly fall apart.

This is where Temporal Context comes in. We architect VLMs in a way that we keep track of:
> Action recognition across time
> Behavioral pattern detection
> Causal reasoning ("this happened because of that")
> Intent and trajectory prediction

Here's how your brain does it:
You walk into a coffee shop. In milliseconds, you're not just detecting objects. You're modeling trajectories, predicting collisions, reading intent, inferring social dynamics.
> "That person is about to bump into you, step aside." 
> "The barista is overwhelmed with orders, lets give them a minute." 
> "That table is about to free up, slowly approach it."

All of this requires time as an input modality.

So time isn't just another dimension. It's THE dimension that separates perception from understanding. And solving it makes AI think in more human terms.

## 7. When people think Video + AI, they think generation, but there's so much more...

When people think Video + AI, they think generation, but there's so much more...

"AI video" doesn't mean what you think it means. When most people hear it, they picture Sora generating movie clips from text. But that's just the tip of the VLM iceberg.

Let me break down the layers to this:

1. Text input → Video output (Generation): You type a prompt, they render out a video. These demos are impressive because they can cheat benchmarks and lean into your confirmation bias of "Hey, that's a pretty good video". But they're mostly limited to ~25 seconds max. They can't generate to specifics of styles and movements. Physics still breaks. And they're not production-ready for most use cases (this means there's still some job security for video editors and creators). Examples that you know of are Sora, Runway, Veo.

2. Video input → Text output (Understanding): This is where things get interesting (at least for me). Feed a model hours of footage. Get structured analysis, summaries, action breakdowns. More than you a regular human could understand themselves. Not taking a podcast and giving you a bite size summary, but taking an MMA fight and understanding body feints, creation of negative spaces, sequence strategies, and application of IQ that veterans may even miss. This is what I'm building.

3. Video inputs → Video outputs (Transformation): Taking video A and asking what would happen if xyz happened to generate video B. This is combining Layer 1 (Generation) and Layer 2 (Understanding) to a different level. Imagine taking the iconic opening scene from Inglorious Basterds and asking how this would be transformed if Bollywood was making it. Or wondering how Real Madrid and FC Barcelona would have changed their game styles if Messi and Ronaldo were to switch sides. This requires deep understanding of concepts with highly specifiable generation capabilities.

4. Video input → Physics outputs (Superhuman Vision): We often take for granted how easily we comprehend what we see. We can gauge depth because we have 2 eyes a specific distance away from each other (stereoscopic vision). We have familiarized understanding of materials, textures and newtonian laws, so we can tell when an overdramatic action scene from an anime or Tamil action movie is actually physically uncanny. Doing this with a computer takes a lot of deep understanding of the world to supplement and cross check with vision. It took video game developers (who all used NVidia graphics cards too) decades to work on physics simulation for elements we are highly accustomed to like water, fire, earth and air, and it's still not perfect. This is a decade long problem to work on, which makes the VLM horizon vast. When we crack this, we can easily reconstruct 3D mental models grounded in real time physics with simple 2D videos.

Currently, we are still in Layer 1 and making some progress on Layer 2. 

Now that you understand the depth of what we can build, I hope it makes you as excited as I am.
